# Editorial Method — Say What You Mean

George Orwell's 1946 essay [“Politics and the English Language”](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/) attacks two linked faults: stale images and vague claims. His practical advice is to choose words for their meaning, cut needless words, prefer active voice, and use ordinary English where it works.

This book applies that advice to the editor's prose. It does not use Orwell as a reason to rewrite the speakers.

## The test

Before an editorial sentence stays, ask:

1. What does it claim?
2. Who does the action?
3. Can a shorter word say the same thing?
4. Can any word be cut?
5. Does a technical term name something exact?
6. What evidence supports the sentence?
7. What does the sentence leave open?

## Keep the exchange visible

A clear sentence can still misrepresent the book if it removes the reply that gave the idea its meaning. Editorial prose must name the speaker, preserve the order of turns, and say what the next turn changes. The exchange between Jamie and Pham comes before the concepts drawn from it.

## Applied examples

| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| “The epistemic convergence of heterogeneous agents creates a resilient truth surface.” | “Agents test the same claim by different routes and compare the results.” |
| “A conclusion is produced through the combination of outputs.” | “The agents compare their results.” |
| “The artifacts constitute a demonstration of the framework.” | “The files become evidence only after someone reads and checks them.” |
| “a multi-frontal concurrent and parallel move upon all of known physics” | In an editorial summary: “He studied several branches of physics at once.” |

The last line comes from the source conversation. It remains unchanged inside a quotation. The shorter version appears only when the editor explains its meaning.

## Words kept on purpose

Plain language does not mean small ideas. This book keeps **epistemology**, **consilience**, and **provenance** where they name different questions:

- How do we know?
- Do different kinds of evidence support the same claim?
- Where did the claim or result come from?

The book defines those words when they first appear. It cuts them when a plain verb will do.

## Final rule

Clarity outranks obedience to any style list. If a short sentence would be false, add the words needed to make it true.
