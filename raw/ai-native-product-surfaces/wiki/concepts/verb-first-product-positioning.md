# Verb-First Product Positioning

**Verb-first product positioning** describes a product by the concrete job it performs before naming its category.

The rule: say what the product does to a specific input, for whom, with what outcome, and what pain it removes.

> We [verb] your [specific input] so you can [desired outcome] without [specific pain].

This is an applied form of [[../../agent-workflows/wiki/concepts/verb-first-knowledge|Verb-First vs Noun-First: Actions Before Labels]], the broader communication rule that actions reveal operational meaning while nouns provide identity and indexing.

## Why noun-first positioning fails

Noun-first descriptions make users decode an abstract category:

> We're building a cloud platform for AI.

That sentence names a market shelf, but it does not show the product doing useful work. `Platform`, `assistant`, `workflow system`, and `infrastructure layer` can each describe many incompatible products.

Verb-first descriptions make the work visible:

> We containerize your code and run it on cloud GPUs so you don't have to manage the infrastructure yourself.

The reader can identify the input, action, environment, and removed pain.

## Required pieces

A useful product sentence answers:

1. **Input:** What does the product touch?
   - code, emails, invoices, calls, PDFs, appointments, leads, reports
2. **Action:** What does it do to that input?
   - summarize, route, generate, detect, schedule, reconcile, convert, monitor, clean, approve
3. **User:** Who receives the value?
4. **Outcome:** What becomes faster, safer, easier, or newly possible?
5. **Removed pain:** What manual work, uncertainty, delay, or infrastructure disappears?

Short form:

> We [verb] [object] so [user] can [result].

## Rewrite trigger

If the first sentence names what the product *is* before saying what it *does*, rewrite it.

Noun-first:

> An AI platform for small businesses.

Verb-first:

> We turn customer emails into draft replies so small teams can answer faster without hiring more support staff.

Noun-first:

> A workflow automation system for agencies.

Verb-first:

> We turn meeting notes into assigned follow-up tasks so agencies do not lose client decisions.

## What difference does it make to people?

Verb-first positioning reduces the amount of market and technical knowledge a reader needs before they can judge relevance. It helps someone answer:

- Is this for me?
- What do I give it?
- What will it do?
- What changes afterward?
- Which current pain might disappear?

The category can still follow. Categories help comparison and discovery; they should not carry the entire explanation.

## Beyond products: capability as action

The same test applies to abstract capability claims such as `taste`, `distribution`, `community`, `brand`, and `strategy`.

- **Taste:** What does someone notice, select, reject, edit, sequence, protect, and explain?
- **Distribution:** How does a person encounter, recognize, try, experience value, return, or share?
- **Community:** Who gathers, contributes, helps, returns, and develops belonging?
- **Brand:** What expectations are repeatedly created and kept?
- **Strategy:** What is chosen, refused, sequenced, and protected under constraints?

Nouns describe the identity someone wants credited. Verbs expose the value they can repeatedly produce.

## Pass/fail test

If someone can reasonably respond with **“What does that mean?”**, the description probably still depends on a category label.

If they can repeat the input, action, user, and result in plain language, the positioning is doing its job.

## Boundary

Verb-first positioning is not a promise that every product is useful. A concrete sentence can still describe weak demand, a poor workflow, or an unproven outcome.
